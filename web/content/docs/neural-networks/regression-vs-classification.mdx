# Regression vs. Classification

Neural networks can solve many problems, but they generally fall into two categories:
1.  **Regression:** Predicting a continuous number (e.g., house price, temperature).
2.  **Classification:** Predicting a discrete label (e.g., cat vs. dog, spam vs. ham).

## What Are We Predicting?

If you are predicting the price of a house, you want a number like `$500,000`.

If you are predicting whether an email is spam, you usually want a probability-like score such as $0.9$, meaning "the model is very confident this is spam."

That difference changes the final layer, the interpretation of the output, and the loss function.

## The Geometric Difference

**Regression** is about closeness on a number line.

If the target is $500000$ and the model predicts $510000$, the prediction is wrong by $10000$. The error has a meaningful distance.

**Classification** is about assigning probability mass to the correct class.

For a cat-vs-dog model, class `cat` is not "numerically close" to class `dog`. The useful question is: how much probability did the model place on the correct class?

## Why MSE Fits Regression

Mean squared error measures numeric distance:

$$
J = \frac{1}{m} \sum (y - \hat{y})^2
$$

Squaring does two useful things:

- large mistakes are punished more than small mistakes
- positive and negative errors do not cancel out

For regression, this matches the task. A prediction of $510000$ is better than $700000$ when the target is $500000$, because it is numerically closer.

## Why Cross-Entropy Fits Classification

Binary cross-entropy is:

$$
J = -\frac{1}{m} \sum (y \log \hat{y} + (1-y) \log (1-\hat{y}))
$$

For multi-class classification:

$$
J = -\sum y_i \log(\hat{y}_i)
$$

The key idea is simple:

> The model is punished for assigning low probability to the correct class.

Suppose the true class is `cat`.

- Predicting $P(\text{cat})=0.9$ gives a small loss.
- Predicting $P(\text{cat})=0.5$ gives a larger loss.
- Predicting $P(\text{cat})=0.01$ gives a very large loss.

That last case matters. Cross-entropy heavily punishes confident wrong predictions, which is exactly what we want in classification.

## Logits, Sigmoid, And Softmax

Neural networks often produce raw scores called **logits** before probabilities.

For binary classification, a sigmoid turns one logit into a probability:

$$
\hat{y} = \sigma(z)
$$

For multi-class classification, softmax turns a vector of logits into a probability distribution:

$$
\hat{y}_i =
\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}
$$

In PyTorch, `nn.CrossEntropyLoss()` expects raw logits, not already-softmaxed probabilities. It applies the stable log-softmax calculation internally.

## Implementation Reading

In PyTorch:
*   **Regression:** `nn.MSELoss()`
*   **Binary classification:** `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()`
*   **Multi-class classification:** `nn.CrossEntropyLoss()`

The "with logits" part means the loss function expects raw scores and handles the sigmoid or log-softmax step in a numerically stable way.

## AI/ML Relevance

Choosing the loss function decides what kind of mistake the model cares about.

- MSE says: "be numerically close."
- Cross-entropy says: "put high probability on the correct class."

That is why the loss must match the task.

## Reader Checkpoint

- **Are we predicting a number or a category?** This determines the output interpretation.
- **Why does MSE fit regression?** Because numeric distance is meaningful.
- **Why does cross-entropy fit classification?** Because probability assigned to the correct class is what matters.
- **Why use logits in PyTorch losses?** Because the built-in loss functions combine activation and log calculations stably.
