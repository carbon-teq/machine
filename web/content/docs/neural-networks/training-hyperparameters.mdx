# Training Hyperparameters

Weights and biases are **parameters**. The model learns them from data.

Hyperparameters are different. They are choices we set from the outside to control how training behaves.

Examples:

- learning rate
- momentum
- weight decay

They do not directly make predictions. They shape the learning process.

## Learning Rate

The **learning rate** controls how big each update step is.

In simple gradient descent:

$$
\theta_{\text{new}}
=
\theta_{\text{old}} - \alpha \nabla L(\theta)
$$

Here $\alpha$ is the learning rate.

Read it like this:

> gradient tells the direction; learning rate decides the step size.

If the learning rate is too small:

- loss decreases very slowly
- training takes too long
- the model may look stuck even though it is improving

If the learning rate is too large:

- loss may jump around
- training may diverge
- the model may overshoot good solutions

### When To Increase Or Decrease

Increase learning rate when:

- loss is decreasing smoothly but painfully slowly
- gradients are healthy but updates are tiny
- training makes almost no progress after many steps

Decrease learning rate when:

- loss explodes
- loss jumps up and down wildly
- validation performance becomes unstable
- training improves at first, then keeps overshooting

In practice, learning rate is usually the first hyperparameter to tune.

## Momentum

Momentum helps gradient descent remember its previous direction.

Without momentum, each update only looks at the current gradient.

With momentum, the optimizer keeps a moving update direction:

$$
v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + (1-\beta)g_t
$$

$$
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \alpha v_t
$$

Here:

- $g_t$ is the current gradient
- $v_t$ is the moving direction
- $\beta$ controls how much past direction is remembered

Momentum is useful when gradients are noisy or zigzag across a valley. It smooths the path and can speed movement in a consistent direction.

## What Is Beta?

Beta usually means a **memory factor**.

If $\beta$ is close to $0$, the optimizer mostly trusts the current gradient.

If $\beta$ is close to $1$, the optimizer remembers the past for longer.

Common momentum value:

$$
\beta = 0.9
$$

This means the optimizer keeps a strong memory of previous update directions.

### When To Adjust Momentum

Increase momentum when:

- updates are noisy
- loss decreases but the path zigzags
- training needs smoother movement through long valleys

Decrease momentum when:

- training overshoots repeatedly
- the optimizer seems to keep moving in a bad direction
- loss reacts too slowly after the gradient direction changes

Most of the time, the default momentum value works well enough.

## Weight Decay

Weight decay discourages weights from becoming too large.

It is a regularization hyperparameter.

The rough idea is:

$$
\text{new loss} = \text{data loss} + \lambda \|\theta\|^2
$$

Here $\lambda$ controls how strongly large weights are punished.

Increase weight decay when:

- training accuracy is high but validation accuracy is poor
- the model is overfitting
- weights are becoming unnecessarily large

Decrease weight decay when:

- the model underfits
- both training and validation performance are poor
- the model seems too constrained

## Quick Tuning Guide

| Symptom | Likely Change |
| :--- | :--- |
| Loss decreases very slowly | Increase learning rate slightly |
| Loss explodes or becomes `nan` | Decrease learning rate |
| Loss zigzags heavily | Try momentum or lower learning rate |
| Training good, validation bad | Add/increase weight decay |
| Training and validation both bad | Train longer, improve model, or reduce regularization |
| Training is noisy but not diverging | Try momentum or lower learning rate |

## Key Idea

Parameters are learned by the model.

Hyperparameters are chosen by you to control learning.

The most important habit is to change one thing at a time, watch the loss curves, and ask:

> Is the model learning too slowly, too wildly, or memorizing too much?
