# Chain Rule for Multivariable Functions

Imagine a factory pipeline:
raw input goes through one machine, then another, then another.
If the final output changes, where did that change come from?

That is exactly the chain rule question in deep learning.

## 1. Why We Need It

In many systems, one variable does not affect the output directly. It affects an intermediate variable, which then affects the output.

Imagine you turn a **Dial ($t$)**.
- The Dial turns a **Gear ($x$)**.
- The Gear moves a **Machine ($z$)**.

So $t \to x \to z$.

If you want to know "How much does the Machine move when I turn the Dial?", you multiply the effects:
$$
\text{Total Effect} = (\text{Machine sensitivity to Gear}) \times (\text{Gear sensitivity to Dial})
$$

This is the Chain Rule.

Now, what if the Dial turns **two** gears ($x$ and $y$), and **both** gears push the Machine?

$$
t \to x \to z
$$
$$
t \to y \to z
$$

You simply add the effects from both paths.

## 2. The Formula

If $z$ depends on $x$ and $y$, and both depend on $t$:

$$
\frac{dz}{dt}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}}_{\text{Path 1}}
+
\underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}}_{\text{Path 2}}
$$

It says: **Total Change = Change via $x$ + Change via $y$**.

## 3. Symbol Dictionary

- $z$: The final output (e.g., Temperature, Cost, Machine Output)
- $x, y$: The intermediate parts (e.g., Position, Neurons, Gears)
- $t$: The input control (e.g., Time, a Dial, a Weight)

## 4. Worked Examples (Two Real Problems)

### Example 1: Temperature changing with position over time

Imagine a room where temperature is not uniform. If you walk, the temperature you feel can change for two separate reasons:

- you move in the $x$ direction, where temperature may rise or fall,
- you move in the $y$ direction, where temperature may also rise or fall.

So your total temperature change is the sum of these two effects:
"change from $x$ motion" + "change from $y$ motion."

Now we write that situation in notation. Suppose temperature in the room is
$$
T(x,y)=x^2+2xy
$$
where $(x,y)$ is your position.

Now imagine you walk on a path:
$$
x(t)=t,\qquad y(t)=t^2
$$

We want $\frac{dT}{dt}$: how fast the temperature you feel changes as you move.

The path picture is:
$$
t \to (x(t),y(t)) \to T(x,y),
$$
so information reaches $T$ through two channels: $t \to x \to T$ and $t \to y \to T$.

First, compute partial derivatives:
$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=2x+2y,\qquad
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}=2x
$$

Then compute path derivatives:
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=1,\qquad
\frac{dy}{dt}=2t
$$

Apply chain rule:
$$
\frac{dT}{dt}
=
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}
+
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}
=
(2x+2y)(1)+(2x)(2t)
$$

Substitute $x=t,\ y=t^2$:
$$
\frac{dT}{dt}
=
(2t+2t^2)+4t^2
=
2t+6t^2
$$

So along this path, the temperature change rate is
$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=2t+6t^2.
$$

### Example 2: Two chained neurons with a cost function

Now let us use a real training setup: we do not optimize with respect to time, we optimize with respect to parameters by minimizing loss.

**The Goal:** We want to decrease the Loss $\mathcal{L}$ (error). To do that, we need to know:
> "If I nudge weight $w_2$ slightly, how much does the Loss $\mathcal{L}$ change?"

In math, this question is exactly the partial derivative $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2}$.

**The Path of Influence:**

1.  **Nudge $w_2$** $\to$ changes the weighted sum $z_2$.
2.  **Change in $z_2$** $\to$ changes the activation $\hat{y}$ (via sigmoid).
3.  **Change in $\hat{y}$** $\to$ changes the Loss $\mathcal{L}$.

Visually:
$$
w_2 \xrightarrow{\text{step 1}} z_2 \xrightarrow{\text{step 2}} \hat{y} \xrightarrow{\text{step 3}} \mathcal{L}
$$

The Chain Rule says the **total sensitivity** is just the product of the **local sensitivities** at each step.

$$
\text{Total Effect} = (\text{Effect of } \hat{y} \text{ on } \mathcal{L}) \times (\text{Effect of } z_2 \text{ on } \hat{y}) \times (\text{Effect of } w_2 \text{ on } z_2)
$$

Or in notation:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z_2} \cdot \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2}
$$

---

**Let's calculate it with real numbers.**

Model equations:
$$
z_1 = w_1 x + b_1,\qquad a_1=\sigma(z_1)
$$
$$
z_2 = w_2 a_1 + b_2,\qquad \hat{y}=a_2=\sigma(z_2)
$$
$$
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{y}-y)^2,\qquad
\sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}.
$$

Let
$$
x=1,\ y=1,\ w_1=2,\ b_1=0,\ w_2=3,\ b_2=0.
$$

**Forward pass (Computing values):**
$$
z_1=2,\quad a_1=\sigma(2)\approx 0.8808
$$
$$
z_2=3(0.8808)\approx 2.6424,\quad
\hat{y}=\sigma(2.6424)\approx 0.9335.
$$

**Backward pass (Computing sensitivities):**

1.  **Effect of $\hat{y}$ on $\mathcal{L}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}} = (\hat{y} - y) = (0.9335 - 1) = -0.0665 $$
    *(The error is negative, so we want $\hat{y}$ to go up)*

2.  **Effect of $z_2$ on $\hat{y}$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z_2} = \sigma'(z_2) = \hat{y}(1-\hat{y}) = 0.9335(1-0.9335) \approx 0.0621 $$
    *(The sigmoid slope at the output)*

3.  **Effect of $w_2$ on $z_2$:**
    $$ \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2} = a_1 = 0.8808 $$
    *(The input strength from the previous layer)*

**Total Gradient for $w_2$:**
Multiply them all together:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2} \approx (-0.0665) \cdot (0.0621) \cdot (0.8808) \approx -0.0036
$$

This tells us: increasing $w_2$ slightly will **decrease** the loss (because the gradient is negative).

---

#### Gradient for hidden-layer weight $w_1$

Now the chain is longer. The influence path is:
$$
w_1 \to z_1 \to a_1 \to z_2 \to \hat{y} \to \mathcal{L}
$$

We just multiply the sensitivities along the whole path:
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}}
\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z_2}
\frac{\partial z_2}{\partial a_1}}_{\text{Backprop from future}}
\cdot
\underbrace{\frac{\partial a_1}{\partial z_1}
\frac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_1}}_{\text{Local steps}}
$$

Notice the first part is just the gradient arriving at $a_1$. We reuse the work we did!

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1} \approx \dots \approx -0.00147
$$

This "reusing the chain" is exactly what Backpropagation is.

> [!TIP]
> **ML Relevance**
> Backpropagation is repeated chain rule. Each layer passes gradient information backward so every parameter gets credit (or blame) for the final error.
