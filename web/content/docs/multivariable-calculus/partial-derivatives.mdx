# Partial Derivatives

Suppose you are standing on a mountain in heavy fog.
You can only take tiny steps, and you need to answer one practical question:
**"Which way should I move to climb fastest?"**

In single-variable calculus, this was easy: there was only one direction to move, so one derivative gave the slope.
Now your height is described by a surface:
$$
z = f(x, y)
$$
and you can move in many directions. So asking for "the derivative" is no longer enough.

We need a more focused question:
**How steep is the surface if we move in one chosen direction while freezing the other?**

## 1. The Slicing Intuition

To make this concrete, we start with the two coordinate directions:
1.  **The X-direction:** Moving purely East/West.
2.  **The Y-direction:** Moving purely North/South.

Imagine taking a giant knife and slicing the mountain:
*   Keep $y$ fixed, and slice in the $x$ direction -> you get a 2D curve.
*   Keep $x$ fixed, and slice in the $y$ direction -> you get another 2D curve.

Now we are back to familiar territory: each slice is a normal 2D curve, and we can measure its tangent slope.
Those two slopes are the partial derivatives.

## 2. Notation

We use a special curly "d" symbol ($\partial$, pronounced "del") to distinguish partial derivatives from normal ones.

*   **$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$**: How much $z$ changes if we wiggle $x$ (holding $y$ constant).
*   **$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$**: How much $z$ changes if we wiggle $y$ (holding $x$ constant).

**Symbol Dictionary**
*   $x, y$: horizontal coordinates (where you are on the map)
*   $z = f(x, y)$: altitude at that location
*   $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$: slopes along the $x$ and $y$ directions

## 3. The Mechanics: Treat Constants as Constants

Calculating partial derivatives is surprisingly easy. You just pretend the other variable is a number (a constant).

**Example:** Let $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^3$.

**Find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$:**
*   We care about $x$.
*   We treat $y$ as a constant (like the number 5).
*   Derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$.
*   Derivative of $y^3$ (which is a constant) is $0$.
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x
$$

**Find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$:**
*   We care about $y$.
*   We treat $x$ as a constant.
*   Derivative of $x^2$ (constant) is $0$.
*   Derivative of $y^3$ is $3y^2$.
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 3y^2
$$

## 4. A Harder Example (Product Rule)

What if they are mixed? Let $f(x, y) = x \cdot y$.

**Find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$:**
*   $y$ is just a coefficient (like $5x$).
*   Derivative of $x$ is $1$.
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 1 \cdot y = y
$$

**Find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$:**
*   $x$ is just a coefficient.
*   Derivative of $y$ is $1$.
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x \cdot 1 = x
$$

> [!TIP]
> **Intuition Check**
> If $z = x \cdot y$, and $y$ is huge (say 100), then a tiny change in $x$ makes a huge change in $z$. That's why the slope w.r.t $x$ depends on $y$!

> [!NOTE]
> **Why this matters in ML**
> In machine learning, the surface is often a **loss landscape**.
> The coordinates are model parameters, and height is prediction error.
> Partial derivatives tell us how error changes when we nudge one parameter at a time, which is exactly what gradient-based training uses.
