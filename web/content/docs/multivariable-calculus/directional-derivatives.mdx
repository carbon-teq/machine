# Directional Derivatives

Partial derivatives answer two axis-aligned questions:
- what happens if I move only in $x$?
- what happens if I move only in $y$?

But in real life, we rarely walk exactly East/West or North/South.
We walk in arbitrary directions, often diagonally.

So we need a new question:
**"What is the slope if I move in this specific direction?"**

## 1. Geometric Intuition

Stand at one point on the surface and pick a direction arrow $\mathbf{u}$.
The **directional derivative** is the slope you feel if you move a tiny amount along that arrow.

It combines local geometry (the gradient) with your chosen direction.

## 2. Formal Notation

For a unit direction vector
$$
\mathbf{u} =
\begin{bmatrix}
u_1 \\
u_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$
the directional derivative is
$$
D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}
$$

Read this as a test for a proposed step direction.

- If $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} > 0$, moving along $\mathbf{u}$ increases the function.
- If $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} < 0$, moving along $\mathbf{u}$ decreases the function.
- If $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$, that direction is locally flat.

So the directional derivative does not just tell us "the slope diagonally." It tells an optimizer whether a chosen direction is useful.

## 3. Symbol Dictionary

- $\nabla f$: gradient vector (points steepest uphill)
- $\mathbf{u}$: unit direction vector (where you choose to move)
- $D_{\mathbf{u}}f$: slope along direction $\mathbf{u}$

## 4. Worked Example

Let
$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2
$$
Then
$$
\nabla f(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
2x \\
2y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

At $(1,1)$:
$$
\nabla f(1,1)=
\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Choose diagonal direction
$$
\mathbf{u}=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then
$$
D_{\mathbf{u}}f
=
\nabla f(1,1)\cdot \mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2 \\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\cdot
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
=
\frac{4}{\sqrt{2}}
=2\sqrt{2}
$$

That number is positive, so this diagonal direction goes uphill.

If we choose the opposite direction,
$$
-\mathbf{u}=
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

then
$$
D_{-\mathbf{u}}f = -2\sqrt{2}
$$

That direction goes downhill at the same local steepness.

This is exactly why gradient descent uses the negative gradient direction. The gradient itself gives the steepest local increase; its opposite gives the steepest local decrease.

> [!NOTE]
> **ML Relevance**
> The gradient gives the best uphill direction, but optimization methods can choose other directions too (for stability, momentum, constraints, or preconditioning). Directional derivatives tell us how good any chosen direction is.

## Reader Checkpoint

Before moving on, you should be able to explain why $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is positive for an uphill step, negative for a downhill step, and largest when $\mathbf{u}$ points in the same direction as the gradient.
