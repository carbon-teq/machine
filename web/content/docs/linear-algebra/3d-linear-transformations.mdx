# 3D Linear Transformations: Beyond the Flatland

We have spent a lot of time on a 2D sheet of paper. But our physical world is 3D. And the world of data? It is often hundreds or thousands of dimensions.

The beauty of linear algebra is that **the rules do not change**. The intuition you built for 2D arrows and grids applies perfectly to 3D space, and even to 1000D space which we cannot visualize.

## The 3D Grid: Adding Depth

In 2D, we tracked two basis vectors, $\hat{\imath}$ (right) and $\hat{\jmath}$ (up).
In 3D, we simply add a third vector: $\hat{k}$, which points **forward** (out of the screen).

Instead of a flat grid, imagine a 3D **lattice**—a jungle gym of cubes filling space. A linear transformation is still a "warp" of this space. It can twist, stretch, or rotate the entire lattice, but it must keep the grid lines parallel and the origin fixed.

## The 3D Matrix

To record a 3D transformation, we just need to track where our three basis vectors land.

*   $\hat{\imath}$ lands at coordinates $\begin{bmatrix} a \\ d \\ g \end{bmatrix}$
*   $\hat{\jmath}$ lands at coordinates $\begin{bmatrix} b \\ e \\ h \end{bmatrix}$
*   $\hat{k}$ lands at coordinates $\begin{bmatrix} c \\ f \\ i \end{bmatrix}$

We pack these three landing spots into a $3 \times 3$ matrix:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}
| & | & | \\
\text{New } \hat{\imath} & \text{New } \hat{\jmath} & \text{New } \hat{k} \\
| & | & |
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b & c \\
d & e & f \\
g & h & i
\end{bmatrix}
$$

When we multiply this matrix by a 3D vector $\mathbf{v}$, we are doing the exact same linear combination as before, just with three parts:

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = x(\text{New } \hat{\imath}) + y(\text{New } \hat{\jmath}) + z(\text{New } \hat{k})
$$

## The Power of N-Dimensions

Here is the secret: **Math doesn't care that you can't visualize 4D.**

In Machine Learning, we rarely work with just 3 dimensions.
*   A user's preference vector might have 50 components (Age, Location, Watch Time, etc.).
*   A language model like GPT uses vectors with **12,288 dimensions** to represent a single word.

We call this **High-Dimensional Space** (or $\mathbb{R}^n$).

Even though we cannot draw a 12,288-dimensional arrow, we treat it **exactly** the same way.
*   It is just a list of numbers.
*   We can add them.
*   We can measure their length (magnitude).
*   We can transform them using a giant matrix.

<Callout title="The Curse of Dimensionality" type="warn">
While the *algebra* works the same, the *geometry* gets weird in high dimensions.
In extremely high-dimensional space, almost all points are "far away" from each other, and the volume of space explodes. This makes finding patterns harder, a problem known as the **Curse of Dimensionality**.
</Callout>

## AI/ML Relevance

<Callout title="Word Embeddings are High-Dimensional Vectors" type="tip">
How does an AI understand the word "King"?
It converts it into a vector, say $\mathbf{v}_{king} \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$.

This vector is a point in a 1024-dimensional space.
Remarkably, the geometry holds meaning. If you take the vector for "King", subtract the vector for "Man", and add the vector for "Woman", you land near the vector for "Queen".

$$ \mathbf{v}_{king} - \mathbf{v}_{man} + \mathbf{v}_{woman} \approx \mathbf{v}_{queen} $$

The AI is performing linear algebra in a "Concept Space" that we can't see, but we can calculate.
</Callout>
